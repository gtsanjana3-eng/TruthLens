Photosynthesis is the biological process by which plants, algae, and certain bacteria convert light energy into chemical energy stored in organic compounds. In its most common form, oxygenic photosynthesis, carbon dioxide and water are used to synthesize carbohydrates, with molecular oxygen released as a by-product; the overall reaction is frequently summarized as six molecules of carbon dioxide plus six molecules of water yielding one molecule of glucose and six molecules of oxygen. The process occurs principally in chloroplasts, organelles containing the green pigment chlorophyll, which absorbs light most strongly in the blue and red regions of the visible spectrum while reflecting green wavelengths. Photosynthesis is conventionally divided into two stages: the light-dependent reactions, which take place in the thylakoid membranes and generate adenosine triphosphate (ATP) and the reduced electron carrier NADPH, and the light-independent reactions of the Calvin–Benson cycle, occurring in the stroma, in which these products drive the fixation of carbon dioxide into three-carbon sugars. Photosynthesis is the primary source of organic matter and free oxygen in the biosphere, and nearly all food chains depend directly or indirectly upon it. The accumulation of atmospheric oxygen produced by early cyanobacteria, an episode known as the Great Oxidation Event roughly 2.4 billion years ago, fundamentally altered Earth's atmosphere and enabled the later evolution of aerobic respiration. Variant pathways have arisen in response to environmental pressures: C4 and CAM photosynthesis concentrate carbon dioxide before fixation, reducing water loss and photorespiration in hot or arid conditions, while anoxygenic photosynthesis in some bacteria uses compounds such as hydrogen sulfide rather than water as an electron donor. Scientific understanding of the process developed through experiments beginning in the seventeenth century, with key contributions from Joseph Priestley, Jan Ingenhousz, Julius von Sachs, and Melvin Calvin.
